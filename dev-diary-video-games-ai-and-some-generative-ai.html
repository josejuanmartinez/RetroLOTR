Field Notes - RetroLOTR Dev Diary

Video Games AI and Some Generative AI

Open a feed today and "AI" means one thing: a model that writes, draws, or talks. Open a game studio's actual engineering requirements and it means pathfinding, behavior trees, utility systems, planners - decades-old techniques that never trend, and that most of a strategy game's "AI" budget still goes to. This is mostly about that kind. It ends with a shorter section on the other kind.

RetroLOTR - a hex-and-card campaign game

RetroLOTR has been my project since I graduated in Video Games Development - the board I've kept building on, and the one I keep bringing new techniques back to as I've picked them up working in the industry since, including time at FRVR and Fortis Games. Most of my own AI experiments land here first, before anywhere else. It's set in Middle-earth's Third Age, the year 2950 - seventeen years before the War of the Ring, while Sauron is still quietly rebuilding in secret - played as one of several Middle-earth factions across a card-driven campaign on a hex map. It's going to be released as open source.

What gets the spotlight

Diffusion models, coding agents, chatbots. Almost everything published about "AI" in games right now is generative - art, dialogue, whole levels.

What studios actually hire for

Search a real studio's AI/gameplay-engineer listing and it's search algorithms, decision trees, planners, and steering - the systems that make an opponent feel like it's playing the game, not writing about it.

We rebuilt both this year. Most of the effort - and most of this article - went into the first kind: replacing the behavior tree driving our AI factions with a genuine hierarchical planner, and rebuilding how every card gets scored, for both the AI and the human player. The second kind gets one section, on purpose, at the end.

Part I

Video Game AI

Behaviour Trees, HTNs, and a Blackboard

Three ideas carry the whole system, and it's worth naming them before going further. A Behaviour Tree is the workhorse of game AI, and has been since roughly Halo 2: a small vocabulary of node types, walked top to bottom, every tick. A Selector tries its children in order and stops at the first that succeeds. A Sequence runs its children in order and stops at the first that fails. Condition and Action nodes are the leaves. A simple strategy-game decision, built out of exactly those pieces, reads almost like a sentence:

a behaviour tree, for illustration
TRYSequence
IFConditionNeedsEconomicHelp IS TRUE
DOActionEconomicAdvisor
OR ELSESequence
IFConditionHasEnemyTarget IS TRUE
DOSelector
TRYActionMilitaristicAdvisor
OR ELSEActionIntelligenceAdvisor
OR ELSEActionMagicAdvisor
OR ELSEActionDiplomaticAdvisor
OR ELSESequence
IFConditionShouldPrioritizeMovement IS TRUE
DOActionMovementAdvisor
OR ELSESelector
TRYActionBestAvailableAction
OR ELSEActionPass

read literally: "if the economy needs help, hand the turn to Economic. Otherwise, if there's an enemy target, try Militaristic; if it has nothing to play, fall to Intelligence, then Magic, then Diplomatic. Otherwise, if nothing urgent is happening, prioritize movement. Otherwise just play the best card, or pass."

It's genuinely fine reasoning, once - and plenty of shipped games never need more than this. The limit is memory: there's no field anywhere holding "what am I in the middle of." A tree like this re-derives "economy needs help, go to Economic" fresh, every single tick, indistinguishable from doing it for the first time, even if the economy dipped into trouble two turns ago and something has been quietly rebuilding it since. It can execute one action per tick, but it can't hold a plan - "fix the economy, then go on the offensive" isn't representable, because a Sequence either finishes in one tick or it doesn't exist.

RetroLOTR's AI leaders need that kind of plan, so they run a genuine Hierarchical Task Network instead - the planning family SHOP2 and Killzone 2's squad AI belong to, not the simplified "priority list" a lot of shipped games settle for. What makes that possible is the third idea: every character the AI controls owns a persistent Blackboard, a small memory that survives from turn to turn, holding a strategy already decomposed into an ordered plan. Where a behaviour tree can only ever re-decide "do Economic" from nothing, the HTN can hold root.recover: build infrastructure until the economy is no longer critical, then - not instead, then - shift into establishing trade until it's stable. A real multi-step sequence, executed over several turns, read back out of the Blackboard rather than re-rolled every time.

The Plan: A Hierarchical Task Network

Concretely, the Blackboard holds a stack of tasks, and the HTN planner is the thing that reads and rewrites that stack every turn. A CompoundTask like root owns an ordered list of Methods, tried top to bottom; the first whose condition holds is the one committed to. A Method decomposes into a sequence of Primitives, run in order, each one biasing the Utility Scorer toward a specific advisor until its own exit condition is met:

ai widget - strategies tab (editor mockup, reproducing the shipped default strategy)
CompoundTaskroot
TRYMethodroot.recoverWHEN EconomyCritical IS TRUE
DOPrimitiveroot.recover.buildbias EconomicUNTIL EconomyWeak
THENPrimitiveroot.recover.tradebias EconomicUNTIL EconomyStable
OR ELSEMethodroot.offenseWHEN HasEnemyTarget IS TRUE
DOCompoundTaskroot.offense.pick
TRYMethod…pick.milWHEN Always
DOPrimitive…mil.leafbias MilitaristicUNTIL Never
OR ELSEMethodroot.movementWHEN ShouldPrioritizeMovement
DOPrimitiveroot.movement.leafbias MovementUNTIL Never
OR ELSEMethodroot.fallbackWHEN Always
DOPrimitiveroot.fallback.leafbias —UNTIL Never

Row order does double duty here: it's the priority a fresh decision is made in (TRY, then OR ELSE, then OR ELSE…) and the order steps execute in inside a Method (DO, then THEN). We didn't have a live Editor session to screenshot for this piece, so this reproduces the actual shipped default strategy field-for-field rather than faking a screen.

Committing to a plan for its own sake would be dangerous: a leader mid-offensive that kept attacking while its treasury quietly hit zero, just because "stay committed to the current task" never re-examines whether staying committed is still smart, isn't a plan worth having. The row order above deliberately does double duty as priority to prevent exactly that: a task ranked higher is always free to interrupt a running lower one, mid-plan, however invested the AI already is; a task ranked lower never can. root.recover, sitting above root.offense in the list, will always break in and take over if the economy collapses - but a marginally-better military opportunity, ranked below whatever's already running, is ignored until the AI is actually done with its current step. Commitment and self-preservation, from one piece of data.

One Blackboard Per Character

Fig. 1 describes a single decision. The full system makes that decision independently for every character an AI leader controls, and a handful of details make that hold up across a whole roster rather than just a single leader in isolation.

Keyed Per Character, Not Per Leader

The Blackboard is keyed per (leader, character), not per leader: AITurnController asks the HTN a fresh question, from that character's own AIContext, once for every character it controls. An Emissary sitting quietly at the capital and a Commander three provinces away standing next to an enemy end up on genuinely different plans - root.diplomacy and root.offense respectively - each fitted to the character holding it, not one plan borrowed from whichever character happened to be evaluated first.

Giving the Plan an Address

An active task can also carry a situational signal - Militaristic.EnemyPressure, say - without that being enough on its own to say where the pressure is coming from. The score goes up correctly, but nothing yet points a character at the hex actually responsible for it. So every preferred parameter that has a location resolves to one:

Travel, Then Act

That target hex is also why turn order runs evaluate, then travel, then act, and never the other way around: acting first from wherever a character already happens to be standing, then wandering only afterward toward whatever looks interesting, would have nothing to do with what was just done. Three steps, run in this order every turn:

Evaluate

The HTN resolves this character's active task from its own Blackboard and, if the task has one, the target hex behind it.

Travel, if needed

If the character isn't already standing on that hex, it moves there first. The cached AIContext is now stale, so it's rebuilt fresh from the character's new position rather than reused.

Act

The full deck is scored and played from where the character actually stands now - not from wherever it started the turn.

A Branch That Wins, But Can't Play

A high-priority branch winning selection doesn't, by itself, guarantee it's the right thing to bias toward: the Utility Scorer always ranks the character's entire deck regardless of which branch is active, so a character is never left doing nothing - it always falls through to whatever it can actually play. But branch selection still needs to know whether a branch's cards are playable at all, or a high-priority branch a character has nothing eligible for would win anyway, and in winning would deny its situational bias to whatever other branch the character could genuinely act on that turn.

This is handled with a role-eligibility check - not a full affordability check, deliberately; a character who's just temporarily short on gold should still be considered eligible - run while walking the tree: at any leaf, AIContext.HasEligibleCard(advisor) asks whether this character has at least one card, anywhere in the deck, whose action resolves to that advisor and is playable by their role. If not, the planner backtracks to the next sibling in priority order, all the way down to the guaranteed fallback if nothing else qualifies.

htn planner - backtracking through root.recover (excerpt)
TRYMethodroot.recover.mithrilWHEN MithrilInsufficient
DOPrimitive…mithril.leafbias EconomicHasEligibleCard fails — skipped
OR ELSEMethodroot.recover.timberWHEN TimberInsufficient
DOPrimitive…timber.leafbias EconomicHasEligibleCard passes — committed

this character has no mithril-related card it can play, so the planner skips the whole mithril branch - not just its leaf - and commits to the next sibling instead, rather than winning the turn's bias with a branch it can never act on.

The Rest of the Map Starts Thinking

Non-playable leaders - Sauron's captains and the other non-player rosters - run through this exact same pipeline: Blackboard, HTN, Utility Scorer, one full character-by-character turn. They don't join the normal per-leader rotation, since that would mean touching the core turn order the campaign game relies on; instead every non-playable leader acts once, in a single batched pass, right after the last playable leader's turn each round and before the next round begins.

turn order - one full round
Player→Competitor 1→Competitor 2→ …
All non-playable leadersbatched, one HTN turn each→Playernext round

They also don't share a deck with any single nation, since a non-playable leader isn't tied to one: each draws instead from one of three alignment decks - Dark Servants, Free Peoples, or Neutral - alongside the same shared Action and Spell pools every playable leader draws from too. Khamul, say, is a Dark Servant regardless of which nation happens to be nearby, and plays accordingly.

An Economy That Knows Its Own Deck

The Economic advisor's "insufficient" and "surplus" signals aren't a flat, identical bar applied to every leader in the game - a leather-heavy Dark Servant roster and a mithril-hungry one don't get held to the same line, because they don't spend the same way.

Each leader's deck is scanned once, when it's built, summing what every card in it actually costs to play, per material, into a target distribution: the share of its total material spend that deck wants to be holding in mithril versus timber versus leather, and so on. That target is cached once per nation and copied down to every character's own Blackboard. Every turn, the leader's actual stockpile share is measured the same way and compared against it - the gap, not the raw amount, is what drives Buy and Sell bias. A mithril-heavy, timber-light deck sells timber to buy mithril, chasing its own deck's mean, not a number picked once for every leader in the game.

Seeing It Work: A Debug Panel

None of the above is worth much if it can only be trusted from reading the code, so it has its own inspector: Ctrl+Tab opens a runtime panel, type a character's name, and it renders that character's Blackboard exactly as the AI itself sees it - live, not a log dump after the fact. Stack, target hex, resource distribution, advisor viability, and the actual candidate cards, side by side, is enough to catch the kind of mismatch that stays invisible without a way to just ask the AI what it's thinking - a correctly-elevated Economic score, say, paired with an empty list where a purchase should have been.

How a Card Actually Gets Chosen: Advisors

The HTN decides what kind of turn this is. It doesn't decide which card gets played - that's a separate system, and it's the one doing the actual arithmetic every turn. Every card's action belongs to one of six advisors - Militaristic, Economic, Diplomatic, Intelligence, Magic, Movement - and every candidate card gets scored, in full, before anything is played:

score = base − difficulty penalty − gold-cost pressure + skill affinity + situational bonus + HTN bias

Difficulty and gold cost push a card down when it's risky or the treasury is thin (the gold penalty scales harder while the economy is already struggling). Skill affinity rewards a card for suiting the character playing it - a high-Agent character leans toward Intelligence-advised cards. The situational bonus is advisor-specific: Militaristic cares about army strength and enemy distance, Diplomatic cares how close an unrevealed NPC is, Economic cares about the current economy tier. And the HTN's currently active task adds one more flat bonus - only to cards whose advisor matches what it's currently biasing toward. It's a nudge, not a filter: a much better off-strategy card can still win the turn.

Attack goes from losing badly (−1.0) to winning decisively (24.0) - not because "Attack is good when there's an enemy" is hardcoded anywhere, but because army strength, enemy distance, and the active strategy all independently pushed the same direction. Sell 5 Iron, last turn's clear winner, drops to a distant third the instant the crisis it was solving is over. Same formula, same cards, opposite outcome, because the world changed.

The scoring also runs against the leader's entire deck now, not just the hand it happens to be holding - the same hand-size limit a human plays under. An AI leader considers every card it still owns, drawn or not, and plays the single best one, up to a difficulty setting of one to four cards per character per turn. It's a deliberate, disclosed asymmetry: the AI is worse than a human at long-range tactics, so we compensate by removing draw luck from its side of the table instead of stacking a second handicap on top of a real one.

Teaching the Scorer: an MLP from the Action Log

There is a natural next experiment here. Every completed AI order is already appended as one JSON object to ai_actions.jsonl, under Unity's persistent data folder. It records the acting leader and character, alignment, active HTN task, skills, army strength, health, resources and income, economy state, nearby threats, target information, the chosen action, its cost and difficulty, every action the Utility AI scored, and the changes observed after execution. Once those records have been reviewed and curated, they can train a small multilayer perceptron, or MLP, to estimate how suitable a playable card is in a particular state.

An MLP is a compact stack of fully-connected numeric layers. It has no plan and no memory of its own - which is useful here, because planning and memory already belong to the HTN and Blackboard. Its job would be narrower: accept a fixed-length description of this state and this candidate card, then return one learned value.

MLP(state before the order, active HTN task, candidate card) → learned candidate value

Turn the log into decision examples

One JSONL line is one decision outcome, but one training example should be one candidate. Each line is therefore expanded into the cards listed in scoredActions; the shared state is copied to each row and joined with that candidate's card data - advisor, action identity, requirements, cost, difficulty, and target distance. The selected card becomes the positive candidate and the alternatives become comparisons. A better future logger would store these candidate fields as structured JSON rather than the current compact action|score|distance strings.

Use only information known before acting

The model input uses the pre* values and pre-order context: skills, health, army strength, resources, income, economy tier, distances, alignment, target type, HTN task, and candidate-card fields. Post-action values and every *Delta are labels or evaluation signals, never inputs. Feeding them into training would leak the answer - the model would appear brilliant offline while knowing something the running game cannot know.

Encode, normalise, and preserve the recipe

Bounded numbers are scaled to stable ranges. Categories such as alignment, economy tier, advisor, card/action identity, and HTN task become one-hot values or small learned embeddings. Missing targets and distances get an explicit presence mask instead of a magic number. The fitted means, scales, category vocabulary, and exact feature order are exported beside the model; runtime inference must apply precisely the same transformation used during training.

Train a small ranking network

A sensible first network is deliberately modest: perhaps 128, 64, and 32 hidden units with ReLU activations, producing one scalar. Curator-approved choices can train a pairwise ranking loss - the approved card should score above a rejected alternative. Immediate resource, army, health, and victory-point deltas can supply a secondary outcome target for actions that were actually executed, but they are not counterfactual evidence for cards that were never played. Class weighting also matters, or common actions such as trading and scouting will overwhelm rare diplomatic or magical decisions.

Split by match, not by line

Rows from the same campaign are highly related, so randomly splitting individual JSON lines would put near-identical turns in both training and validation. Whole matches, seeds, or save sessions should be held out together; adding a stable matchId to future log entries makes this reliable. I would compare pairwise accuracy, top-choice accuracy, calibration, and full simulated-game outcomes against both the existing Utility scorer and a simple linear baseline. If the MLP cannot beat the simpler baseline on unseen matches, it has not earned a place in the game.

The curation is the important part. Training only on the action the existing scorer chose would mostly teach the network to imitate the existing formula, including its mistakes. Useful records need either an explicit human judgement - approved choice, preferred alternative, or rejection reason - or a trustworthy longer-horizon result. The immediate deltas in the current log can say that a trade gained gold or an attack changed army strength; they cannot, by themselves, say whether that choice lost the campaign twelve turns later. Keeping those two kinds of supervision separate prevents a short-term reward from quietly becoming the entire strategy.

offline training record - one decision expanded by candidate
Statepre-resources + skills + army + distances + economy
Planalignment + active HTN task + preferred target
Candidatecard/action + advisor + requirements + cost + difficulty
Labelcurator preference; observed outcome only where executed

At runtime the network should remain a scorer, not become a second game controller. The existing rules first remove dead, unaffordable, illegal, or otherwise unplayable cards. The Blackboard and HTN still resolve the active strategy and target. The remaining candidates are encoded as one batch and passed through the exported ONNX model using the Unity Inference package already present in the project. A network this small is cheap enough to run on the CPU, especially when the world-state portion of the feature vector is cached once at the beginning of the turn.

final score = authored Utility score + α × learned MLP score

I would ship that gradually. First run the MLP in shadow mode: log its ranking and model version, but let the authored scorer continue choosing. Then use it only to break close ties. Only after held-out matches and automated campaigns show a consistent improvement should α rise enough for it to reorder clear Utility choices. The playable-card gate remains absolute, the difficulty setting still caps orders, and a missing model or malformed feature vector falls back to the deterministic scorer. The result is not an opaque neural agent replacing the game AI; it is one learned opinion inside an accountable system whose plan, legal moves, fallback, and final arithmetic can still be inspected.

Situations, and the Cards Offered to You

All of the above runs for AI leaders. The human player gets a related but distinct system: Opportunity Cards - a handful of situational cards surfaced automatically when your character walks into a hex that matters (an army sitting outside an enemy PC, a hostage nearby, an unrevealed NPC in range - about twenty such conditions are tracked).

Worth being precise about what that actually looks like on screen, because it's easy to picture wrong: it is not a hand of illustrated cards fanning out. What actually appears is a small bloom of compact icons, clustered right next to your character's token, directly on the hex - one glyph per matching situation, no card art at all. Only once you open the bloom do the real, playable cards resolve.

Which situations make it into that bloom is decided by score, not a fixed hand-authored priority order. Each situation still carries a hand-tuned rank, drag-to-reorder in the AI Widget - but that rank now feeds a numeric bonus (ten minus position) into the scoring pass below, rather than acting as a hard cutoff that lets only the single top-ranked match through:

ai widget - situations tab (ranking, excerpt)
1Character at Underground Hex+10
2Army at Enemy PC+9
3Agent at Enemy PC+8
4Emissary at Enemy PC+7
5Army at Enemy PC or Enemy Army+6

Scored and ranked

The whole eligible pool - situation matches, plus Spells and Events - is scored together. The top five, by relevance to this character, fill the bloom; two cards can share the same situation and both make the cut.

The score itself is deliberately simpler than the AI's - no HTN, no advisor bias, since a human isn't following a machine-authored strategy. It's a base score, a small bonus for skills the card actually needs, and the same situational ranking above turned into a numeric gradient instead of a gate. A card requiring high Commander skill scores higher in the hands of a character who actually has it; a card matching the top-ranked active situation scores higher than one matching the fifth.

The Smaller Machines

Not everything that moves in RetroLOTR is "AI" in the decision-making sense, and it's worth being honest about which pieces are and which just look like it from a distance:

  • Real searchCharacter and army movement runs on a genuine A* search over the hex grid - open/closed sets, a proper admissible hex-distance heuristic, and a real per-tile cost function that accounts for terrain and, for armies, warships needed to cross water. The same pathfinder renders the human player's movement preview and feeds AI movement decisions; a Dijkstra-style flood fill sitting next to it answers "what can I reach this turn" for the range highlight.
  • Not AIEncounter cards resolve for AI actors with a flat random pick among the available options - no evaluation of which outcome is better, on purpose. Some moments in the game are meant to be luck, not judgment.
  • Not AINation starting positions come from deterministic procedural generation: a flood fill grows each nation's territory outward from its seed, and a greedy spacing pass keeps capitals from landing on top of each other. Same map shape, same seed, same result - which is exactly what you want from map generation.
Part II

Some Generative AI

This is the short part, on purpose - the kind of AI everyone's already reading about. Our actual problem was never "can we generate fantasy art," that part is easy now. It was consistency: hundreds of cards, painted over years, that all needed to belong to the same visual world, including the hundreds still unpainted. Commissioning our way there wasn't realistic. So instead of prompting a general model toward a named artist, I built a small reference set around the material language I wanted the game to retain and trained a local adapter from that.

And yes, I know generative AI is getting a lot of hate. That is part of why I tried to train my own model rather than ask for anyone's individual design or signature style. What I was after was a visual language typical of an era, much of it produced by technical restrictions as much as personal taste: black ink, old and laser printers, limited colours, hand-drawn lines, hard-edged squares, halftones, and the physical character of inexpensive print. I also do not pretend that this makes the underlying model culturally blank. A foundation model has already learned from an enormous body of visual work; even when I do not explicitly call on a particular influence, that learned material is still there. Training my own LoRA gives me more control and makes my immediate references deliberate, but it does not erase that underlying reality.

Scan the source

We started from our own shelf of old, out-of-print strategy-game books and boxes - the actual physical illustrations this project has always drawn its visual language from - and scanned them at high resolution as the raw reference set.

Paint and correct

Scans are never clean: foxed paper, faded ink, compression artifacts from decades-old printing. A painter went back over every scan by hand, correcting and repainting damage so the training set was made of genuinely good paintings, not degraded photographs of them.

Train the LoRA

Those corrected paintings became the training set for a LoRA fine-tuned on top of Qwen Image - an open-weight, open-source model, trained and run entirely locally, with nothing shipped off to a third-party API for this part of the pipeline. A LoRA is a small, targeted adjustment layered on top of that base model, tuned specifically to reproduce this house style: its palette, its linework, the particular way it paints faces and cloth and stone.

Paint everything, forever

Every card illustrated since is generated through that trained adapter and then judged against the same art direction. The point is not that the foundation model arrived without prior visual knowledge; it plainly did not. The point is that the additional training, the selected references, and the final decisions are local, narrow, and directed toward a period vocabulary rather than the identity of a particular artist.

Building the reference set

I collected and scanned vintage fantasy material to study the visual qualities I wanted the project to carry forward: dense black linework, elaborate armor, dramatic silhouettes, and the tactile character of older print illustration. These cleaned examples show the starting point before repainting.

Then I started to paint some

Before training the model, I started experimenting directly on the black-and-white source: preserving the original ink while introducing small, concentrated areas of colour. These were early studies rather than finished card art, but they helped establish what should survive into the painted house style.

Then I continued painting them completely

The selective-colour studies were only a first step. I moved on to fully painting the source images, rebuilding colour, light, atmosphere, and surface texture across the entire frame while preserving the composition and drawing underneath.

I kept going until I had a small but coherent set of roughly 20–25 fully painted images. Those finished paintings—not the raw scans or the early experiments—became the training set for the RetroLOTR LoRA.

Conclusion

The Useful Kind of AI Is the Kind You Can Account For

These two halves of RetroLOTR use very different things that happen to share the same name. The campaign AI searches, remembers, plans, scores, and commits. The image pipeline learns a narrow visual direction from a small body of work I prepared by hand. Neither is interesting because it is called AI. Each is useful because its role is specific, inspectable, and subordinate to the game I am trying to make.

The planner did not remove authored design; it gave that design memory and structure. The LoRA did not remove painting; it grew out of scanning, correcting, repainting, curating, and deciding what belonged. Nor did training it locally somehow erase the visual inheritance already embedded in the foundation model. In both cases the difficult work was defining the boundaries: what the system may choose, what it must preserve, what remains deliberately random, and where a human decision is still the point.

That is the version of AI in games I find worth building: not a promise that the machine will make the game for us, but a collection of understandable tools that let a carefully made game become deeper, more coherent, and more itself.

Where we drew the line. Every still image in RetroLOTR - every card, every tile - is made through the locally trained LoRA described above, using the small painted and corrected reference set I assembled to steer a pre-trained foundation model. That foundation is not neutral or free of prior visual learning, and I do not want "trained locally" to imply otherwise. The only additional third-party model used in the production pipeline is for motion: turning finished stills into short animated video with Flux 3. I wanted to state those boundaries plainly rather than let "AI-assisted art" cover for something vaguer.
